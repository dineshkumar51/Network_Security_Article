What is encryption?

Encryption turns readable data into an unreadable form, so that only someone with the right key can read it.

  • Ciphertextis the scrambled version. Anyone can see it, but without the key it looks like random noise.
  • A keyis the secret value used to scramble and unscramble the data.

Encryption turns plaintext into ciphertext using a key; decryption turns it back.

Private Integrity Identity

Every time you log in, shop or send a message online, your data passes through networks run by people you don't know. Encryption keeps it private (no one else can read it), protects its integrity (any tampering is detected), and confirms identity (you're talking to the real website, not an impostor).

Symmetric encryption

One key locks, the same key unlocks

Symmetric encryption uses one shared key to both lock and unlock a message. It works like a house key: the same key locks the door and opens it, and anyone with a copy can do both.

plaintext → [ key ] → ciphertext → [ same key ] → plaintext

The Caesar cipher: one of the oldest examples

Around 50 BC, Julius Caesar protected his military messages with a simple trick: he shifted every letter three places along the alphabet. A became D, B became E, C became F, and so on, wrapping around at the end, so X became A.

Here the key is the number 3. Anyone who knows it can shift every letter back by 3 and read the message. Anyone who doesn't sees only gibberish.

    Breaking it

    The Caesar cipher has a fatal flaw: there are only 25 possible keys. An attacker doesn't need to know the key; they can simply try every shift and read whichever one makes sense. This is called a brute-force attack.

    25KEYS

    The lesson that every modern cipher is built on: the number of possible keys must be so huge that trying them all is impossible.

    Modern symmetric encryption: DES and AES

    Today's ciphers work on the same principle as Caesar's (one shared secret key), but they scramble data in far more complex ways and use enormous keys.

    AES replaced it, chosen after an open worldwide competition. It scrambles data in 128-bit blocks, using keys of 128, 192 or 256 bits, through 10 to 14 rounds. It's built into almost every modern processor, and it protects HTTPS, Wi-Fi, disk encryption and messaging apps.

    Key size: 128, 192 or 256 bits

    How computers do it: XOR

    Computers encrypt bits. XOR compares two bits: the same gives 0, different gives 1. XOR with the key once to encrypt, and XOR with the same key again to get the original back. Modern ciphers like AES are far more elaborate, scrambling 128-bit blocks through 10 or more rounds, but the principle is the same.

    0 ⊕ 1 = 1

    DES's 56-bit key gave 256, about 72 quadrillion, possibilities, and it fell to brute force in 1998. AES-128 has 2128, about 3.4 × 1038. A computer testing a quintillion keys per second would need about 10.8 trillion years, roughly 780 times the age of the universe. And with hardware support, AES encrypts several gigabytes per second on a single core.

    So by 1977 we had encryption that was fast and practically unbreakable. Problem solved? Not quite.

    The fatal weakness: sharing the key

    To communicate secretly, both sides need the same key. But how do you send the key? If you already had a secure way to send it, you wouldn't need encryption. For centuries the answer was physical: couriers, diplomatic pouches, codebooks. Even the 1963 Washington–Moscow hotline relied on one-time pad tapes delivered by hand through embassies.

    You can't send a courier to Amazon before buying a book. For the internet to exist, strangers needed a way to agree on a secret in public. For 2,000 years, everyone assumed that was impossible.
    2

    The story: how primes saved the internet

    From an absurd idea to the foundation of online trust

    1976

    A radical idea

    Whitfield Diffie and Martin Hellman proposed using one key to lock and a different key to unlock. Publish the locking key to the world, keep the unlocking key to yourself. Brilliant, but they had no practical way to build it.

    1977

    RSA was born

    Rivest, Shamir and Adleman, three researchers at MIT, spent months trying to make Diffie and Hellman's idea work. In April 1977 they succeeded, and named the result after their initials: RSA. That August, Scientific American announced it to the world.

    1995

    RSA meets the web

    Netscape built RSA into SSL, the protocol it created to protect credit card numbers in its web browser. For the first time, ordinary people could shop online securely, and the idea behind every "https" link was born.

    2000

    Free for everyone

    RSA was patented in the US in 1983, so anyone who wanted to use it had to pay a licence fee. On 6 September 2000, two weeks before the patent was due to expire, RSA Security released it into the public domain. From then on anyone could use it for free, and it quickly spread into browsers, servers and open-source software everywhere.

    The intuition behind asymmetric encryption

    Padlocks and a trapdoor

    The padlock

    Imagine you hand out open padlocks to anyone who asks. Anyone can snap one shut on a box, but only you hold the key that opens it. The open padlock is your public key; the key is your private key. Now strangers can send you secrets without ever meeting you.

    Strangers sharing a secret

    Step through how Alice sends Bob a secret while Eve watches everything on the network.

    The trapdoor

    RSA rests on an asymmetry. Multiplying two primes is instant. Going backwards, finding the two primes that made a number, gets dramatically harder as the number grows. The primes are the trapdoor: whoever knows them can walk back through the door; everyone else is stuck.

    A quick math refresher

    Two clock ideas the math relies on

    Before the math, two quick ideas. RSA does all of its arithmetic on a clock, and it relies on a way to "undo" multiplication there.

    1. What "mod" means

    You already do modular arithmetic every day. If it's 10 o'clock and you add 5 hours, you don't say "15 o'clock," you say 3 o'clock. The clock wraps around after 12.

    That's all "mod" means: arithmetic on a clock with m positions, numbered 0 to m − 1. When you go past the top, you wrap around and keep counting.

    15 mod 12 = 315 is one full lap of 12, plus 3. Equivalently, "15 mod 12" is the remainder when you divide 15 by 12.

    Different numbers can land on the same position. 3, 15 and 27 all land on 3 on a 12-clock, because they differ by whole laps. Mathematicians write this with a special equals sign:

    15 ≡ 3 (mod 12)Read as "15 is congruent to 3, mod 12," which simply means "same position on the clock."

    2. The inverse: undoing multiplication

    With ordinary numbers, every number except 0 has a multiplicative inverse: the number you multiply by to get back to 1.

    a × a−1 = 1,    so    a−1 = 1 / a

    The inverse of 3 is 1/3, because 3 × 1/3 = 1. That's what lets you undo multiplication: 7 × 3 = 21, and 21 × 1/3 = 7, back where you started.

    On a clock, there are no fractions, only whole positions. So 1/3 doesn't exist. Instead, the modular inverse of a is a whole number d that lands on position 1 when multiplied by a:

    a × d ≡ 1 (mod m),    meaning    a × d = k × m + 1a × d must be whole laps of the clock, plus exactly one step.

    On a 10-clock, the inverse of 3 is 7, because 3 × 7 = 21 = 2 laps of 10 + 1. And it undoes multiplication exactly like 1/3 does: 4 × 3 = 12, which lands on 2; then 2 × 7 = 14, which lands on 4, back where you started.

    The one rule: no shared factors. Why does 4 fail on a 10-clock? Because 4 and 10 are both even. Every multiple of 4 is even, and every whole lap of 10 is even, so wherever you land is even too: 0, 2, 4, 6 or 8. Position 1 is odd, so you can never reach it. That's the rule for every clock:

    a has an inverse mod m  ⟺  gcd(a, m) = 1

    In words: an inverse exists exactly when a and m share no common factor. And when it exists, there's exactly one on the clock.

    Two things stand out. Creating a key is slow, but it happens once. And after that, encryption is cheap while decryption is the expensive step: every decryption or signature costs around a millisecond of heavy arithmetic. Keep that number in mind; it's why, as we'll see shortly, RSA is far too slow to encrypt large amounts of data.

    The same math explains why bigger keys are a good deal for defenders. Doubling the key from 1024 to 2048 bits makes decryption about 8× slower for you, but makes factoring roughly a billion times harder for an attacker.

    7

    Why RSA alone isn't enough

    Brilliant for keys and signatures, terrible for bulk data

    1. Every decryption is heavy math. Each one is a big-number modular exponentiation, costing around a millisecond.
    2. Bulk data would crawl. Encrypting large amounts of data with RSA would run on the order of ten thousand times slower than AES.
    8

    Hybrid encryption: the perfect balance

    Use each tool for what it does best

    So far we've met two kinds of encryption, and each has one big weakness:

    • Symmetric encryption (AES) is fast, but both sides need the same key, and there's no safe way to share it with a stranger.
    • Asymmetric encryption (RSA) solves key sharing, but it's slow and can lock only a tiny amount of data at a time.

    The real-world solution is to use both, each for the job it does best:

    1. Create a random AES key, a fresh secret just for this conversation.
    2. Share the key: lock only the AES key with RSA, using the recipient's public key, and send it. That's one slow operation on just 32 bytes.
    3. Transfer the data: lock the data with AES and send it. It's fast, so the data can be any size.

    The recipient unlocks the AES key with their private key, then uses it to unlock the data.

    Envelope encryption

    Step through how HTTPS sets up a secure connection: share the key first, then encrypt the data.

    This is how most secure communication works: secure email, cloud storage and, in a slightly evolved form, every HTTPS website you visit. Modern HTTPS agrees on the AES key using a newer key-exchange method instead of locking it with RSA, but the principle is the same: slow public-key math once, then fast symmetric encryption for everything else. Part 9 shows exactly how.

    9.1

    Certificates: how a website proves who it is

    Before the handshake, one question: whose public key is it?

    RSA lets anyone lock a message using a website's public key. But how does your browser know the public key it received really belongs to bank.example, and not to an attacker pretending to be the bank? That's the job of a certificate.

    What a certificate is

    A certificate is a website's passport. It says "I am bank.example, and this is my public key," along with who issued it and when it expires. Anyone can read it; nothing in it is secret. What makes it trustworthy is a digital signature from a certificate authority.

    Who certificate authorities are

    A certificate authority (CA) is an organization that browsers and operating systems trust to vouch for websites. Only a few dozen exist worldwide, such as Let's Encrypt, DigiCert, Sectigo and Google Trust Services. To be trusted, a CA must pass strict independent audits every year and follow rules agreed between browsers and CAs. If it breaks those rules, browsers stop trusting it.

    How a CA checks that you own the domain

    1. The website creates a key pair and sends the CA a request containing its public key and its domain name. The private key never leaves the website's server.
    2. The CA sets a challenge that only the domain's real owner could complete, for example:
      • A file check: place a specific file at a specific address on the website
      • A DNS check: add a specific record to the domain's DNS settings
      • An email check: click a link sent to an address like admin@bank.example
    3. For higher-assurance certificates, the CA also checks that the organization behind the website is real, using business registries, legal documents or a phone call.

    How the CA signs the certificate

    The CA takes a fingerprint of all the certificate's details, then signs it with its private key: s = hd mod n. Your browser later computes the same fingerprint itself and unseals the signature with the CA's public key: se mod n. If they match, the certificate is genuine, and if anyone changes even one letter, they won't.

    Why three levels?

    1. Protecting the most valuable key: a root key can vouch for any website, so it's kept offline and used only rarely. Intermediates do the day-to-day signing instead.
    2. Limiting the damage: if an intermediate goes wrong, it can be revoked and replaced without updating the root on billions of devices.
    3. Flexibility: CAs run separate intermediates for different purposes and algorithms, and replace them every few years.

    How your browser checks the chain

    Your browser verifies the leaf's signature with the intermediate's public key, then the intermediate's signature with the root's public key, until it reaches a root it already trusts. It also checks that the certificate hasn't expired, that it lists the domain you typed, and that it hasn't been revoked.

    9.2

    Server authentication

    Proving the server really holds the private key

    Server authentication is how your browser makes sure it's talking to the real bank.example. It has two parts: checking the certificate, which we've just covered, and a live proof that the server actually holds the certificate's private key. That second part happens during the handshake.

    1. The problem: a certificate is public. Anyone, including an attacker, can download the bank's real certificate in one click and present it as their own. If showing a certificate were enough, they would pass every certificate check from 9.1.
    2. The solution: during the handshake, the server signs a fingerprint of the handshake so far, including your browser's fresh random number, with its private key. In TLS 1.3, this is the CertificateVerify message. Your browser checks the signature with the public key from the certificate. Because the fingerprint is unique to this connection, a copied signature won't match, and the attacker can't create a new one without the bank's private key.
    9.3

    SSL: the first attempt

    1995 to 1996, designed by Netscape

    In the mid-1990s Netscape wanted people to buy things online, which meant sending card numbers across a network where every router along the way could read them. SSL, the Secure Sockets Layer, slid in between the application and TCP, so any protocol could be secured without being rewritten. HTTP sent through SSL became HTTPS.

    SSL introduced the idea every later version kept: use slow public-key cryptography once to agree on a secret, then switch to fast symmetric encryption for the actual data. In SSL 3.0 the browser invents a random pre-master secret, locks it with the server's RSA public key, and sends it. Both sides mix that secret with two random numbers to derive session keys, then exchange a hash of the whole conversation so any tampering shows up.

    9.4

    TLS 1.0 to 1.2: two decades of patches

    1999 to 2008, standardized by the IETF

    A new tool: elliptic curves and ECDHE

    Elliptic-curve cryptography (ECC) is another trapdoor, similar to RSA. Your private key is a secret number, k. Your public key is where you land after "hopping" k times from a fixed, public point G on a curve. Hopping forward is easy; working out the number of hops from where you landed is practically impossible. And it's compact: a 256-bit ECC key is about as strong as a 3072-bit RSA key.

    ECDHE uses this to let two strangers create the same secret without ever sending it:

    1. The browser picks a secret a, hops a times from G, and sends where it landed: A.
    2. The server picks a secret b, hops b times from G, and sends B.
    3. Each side hops its own secret number of times from what it received. Both land on the same spot: the shared secret.

    Everyone can see A and B, but no one can work out a or b. The secrets are new for every connection and thrown away afterwards, which gives forward secrecy, and the shared secret becomes the AES keys that encrypt the data.

    Agreeing on a secret, in paint

    Mixing paint is easy; unmixing it is practically impossible, just like hopping on a curve.

    In 1999 the IETF took over the protocol and renamed it Transport Layer Security. TLS 1.0 was essentially SSL 3.1, with proper HMAC integrity checks and better key derivation. TLS 1.1 (2006) gave every record its own random IV, closing the hole the BEAST attack later exploited in TLS 1.0. TLS 1.2 (2008) brought SHA-256 and AEAD ciphers such as AES-GCM, which encrypt and authenticate data in one well-designed step.

    Two ways to agree on a key

    With RSA key exchange, the browser encrypts the secret with the server's long-term key, exactly as SSL did. If that key is stolen years later, every recorded session can be decrypted. With ECDHE, both sides create temporary key pairs, compute the same secret without ever sending it, and throw the temporary keys away afterwards. That property is called forward secrecy. The certificate's key now only signs the server's temporary key, so RSA's job moves from encrypting to signing.

    TLS 1.0 and 1.1 were formally deprecated in 2021. TLS 1.2 is still safe when limited to ECDHE and AEAD ciphers; the trouble was everything else it allowed.

    9.5

    TLS 1.3: keep only what's safe

    2018 to today

    Instead of patching again, TLS 1.3 deleted every feature that had ever caused trouble and redesigned the handshake around a simple guess.

    One round trip
    The browser guesses which key exchange the server accepts and sends its key share in the very first message. The guess is almost always right, so data flows a full round trip sooner than with TLS 1.2.
    Forward secrecy for everyone
    RSA key exchange is gone. Every connection uses temporary Diffie–Hellman keys, so a stolen server key can never unlock past traffic.
    Five cipher suites
    CBC mode, RC4, 3DES, MD5, SHA-1, compression and renegotiation were removed. What remains is all AEAD.
    Encrypted certificates
    Everything after ServerHello is encrypted, so observers can no longer read the certificate.
    An explicit signature
    In CertificateVerify the server signs a hash of the entire handshake, binding its identity to this exact connection.
    Faster comebacks
    Returning clients can resume with a ticket and even send data in their first message (0-RTT), as long as that data is safe to replay.
    Ready for quantum computers
    Since 2024, major browsers combine X25519 with the post-quantum ML-KEM, so traffic recorded today stays protected against future quantum computers.

    One detail shows how hard the internet is to change. Firewalls and proxies broke on anything unfamiliar, so TLS 1.3 claims to be TLS 1.2 on the wire, sends a meaningless ChangeCipherSpec message as camouflage, and tucks its real version number into an extension.

    Where RSA lives in TLS 1.3

    RSA no longer encrypts anything in TLS 1.3. The shared secret comes from elliptic-curve key exchange, now combined with post-quantum ML-KEM. RSA's job is purely authentication: signatures on the certificate chain, made by CAs (often RSA); the live CertificateVerify signature, when the website has an RSA certificate (RSA-PSS); client certificates in mutual TLS; and signed revocation information published by CAs.

    Elliptic curves create the secret. RSA vouches for who you created it with.

    9.6

    Mutual TLS: both sides show ID

    A mode you can switch on, not a new version

    Mutual TLS (mTLS) is the TLS handshake we've just seen, with one addition: the client proves its identity too. The differences:

    1. The server asks for a certificate: along with its own certificate, it sends a CertificateRequest message.
    2. The client sends its own certificate, usually issued by the organization's own private CA rather than a public one.
    3. The client proves it holds the private key, by signing the handshake with its own CertificateVerify message, exactly as the server does in 9.2.
    4. No passwords or API keys travel over the network. The client's private key never leaves the client, and the server knows exactly which service or device it's talking to before any data flows.

    Where you'll meet it: service-to-service traffic in microservices and service meshes, partner and payment APIs, IoT devices, and zero-trust networks.

    Putting it all together: the eMudhra incident

    A real case where the math held, but the key didn't

    Everything rests on one promise

    Look back at everything we've covered, and one promise holds it all together: private keys are secret, and impossible to guess.

    • Encryption works because only the right key can unlock the data.
    • Asymmetric encryption works because the public key can be shared while the private key stays secret.
    • The trapdoor works because going backwards is hopeless when the secret is huge: two 309-digit primes, a 617-digit n.
    • Certificates and the chain of trust work because only a CA can produce its signature.
    • TLS works because only the real website can prove it holds its private key.

    In October 2026, that promise broke at a real certificate authority. Let's use everything we've learned to see what happened.

    What happened

    eMudhra is a certificate authority trusted by every major browser. On 17 October 2026, a researcher found that the private keys of eight of its intermediate CA certificates could be worked out from the public certificates themselves. These were the keys eMudhra used to sign certificates for websites and software. Browsers blocked them within hours.

    A different trapdoor, the same idea

    eMudhra's keys used elliptic-curve cryptography (ECC), a newer alternative to RSA. Instead of multiplying two primes, it works by hopping around a curve:

    • The private key is a secret number, k.
    • The public key is where you land after starting at a fixed, well-known point, G, and hopping k times.
    • Hopping forward is easy. Working out how many hops it took, just from where you landed, is practically impossible, as long as k is huge. It should be a random number about 116 digits long.

    It's the same trapdoor as RSA: easy one way, hard the other, and only as strong as the secret is big.

    The bug, in plain words

    Imagine a vault with a 116-digit combination lock, but the combination was set to a number between 1 and 255. The lock is as strong as ever; you just have very few combinations to try.

    That's what happened. A mistake in eMudhra's key-generation script created a second, weak set of keys, and because the two had similar names, the weak keys were used by mistake. Every one of the eight private keys was a number below 256. One of them was simply 4.

    Remember the Caesar cipher from Part 1? It was easy to break because it had only 25 possible keys, so an attacker could simply try them all. eMudhra's keys had 255. Same problem, nearly 2,000 years later.

      Connecting the dots

      With one of these keys, an attacker could undo every protection we've talked about. Here's how each idea from the session is affected:

      What we learnedWhat a leaked CA key means
      Private keys must stay secret (Parts 3 to 5)The key wasn't secret: anyone could compute it in milliseconds.
      The trapdoor needs a huge secret (Parts 3 and 5)A tiny secret means no trapdoor: just try every possibility, like the Caesar cipher.
      Certificates are signed by a trusted CA (Part 9.1)The attacker could sign a valid certificate for any website, such as bank.example.
      The server proves it holds its key (Part 9.2)With his own certificate, the attacker holds the matching private key, so this check passes too.
      Hybrid encryption keeps the data private (Part 8)The attacker becomes the other end of the conversation, so strong AES encryption protects your data, all the way to him.

      What saved the day

      • Certificate Transparency: every website certificate must be publicly logged, and none were found for these keys, so there's no evidence browsers were attacked.
      • Fast blocking: Chrome and Firefox blocked the keys within hours, and browsers went on to remove trust in eMudhra's root above them.
      • The gap: many non-browser clients, such as API clients, scripts and containers, skip these checks and stayed exposed until their trust stores were updated.

      Three lessons to take away

      1. The math wasn't broken; the key was. A strong lock with a guessable combination isn't a strong lock.
      2. One weak link affects everyone, because any trusted CA can sign for any website.
      3. Keep trust stores updated, and never disable certificate checks, especially in backend services and tools.
      Prime numbers and elliptic curves give us locks that would take longer than the age of the universe to pick. But a lock is only as strong as its key: the math secures the internet, and careful engineering keeps it secure.

      0What we will prove

      The RSA setup.
      1. Choose two distinct primes p and q, and let n = p·q.
      2. Let φ(n) = (p − 1)(q − 1).
      3. Choose e with 1 < e < φ(n) and gcd(e, φ(n)) = 1.
      4. Let d be the number with 0 < d < φ(n) and e·d ≡ 1 (mod φ(n)).
      5. Public key: (n, e). Private key: d.
      6. Encrypt a message m, with 0 ≤ m < n, as c = me mod n. Decrypt as cd mod n.
      Theorem (RSA correctness). For every integer m with 0 ≤ m < n:
      (me mod n)d mod n = m

      Before proving it, we need to show that a suitable d always exists (Section 3) and collect the facts the proof uses. Each one is proved from scratch, so nothing is taken on faith.

      1Notation: divisibility and congruence

      Definition (divisibility). For integers a and b, we write a ∣ b ("a divides b") if b = a·k for some integer k.
      Definition (congruence). a ≡ b (mod m) means m ∣ (a − b): a and b leave the same remainder when divided by m. In clock terms, they land on the same position of an m-position clock. We write a mod m for the remainder itself, a number from 0 to m − 1.
      Lemma 1 (arithmetic on the clock). If a ≡ b (mod m) and c ≡ d (mod m), then
      a + c ≡ b + d,   a·c ≡ b·d,   ak ≡ bk (mod m) for every k ≥ 1
      Proof. Write a = b + m·s and c = d + m·t. Then a + c = (b + d) + m(s + t), and a·c = b·d + m(b·t + d·s + m·s·t). Both differences are multiples of m. The power rule follows by applying the product rule k times. ∎

      Why it matters: we can reduce mod n at every step of a calculation without changing the final answer. In particular, decrypting c = me mod n gives cd ≡ (me)d = med (mod n). So it's enough to prove med ≡ m (mod n).

      2The division algorithm and gcd

      Theorem 2 (division algorithm). For integers a and b > 0, there are unique integers q and r with a = b·q + r and 0 ≤ r < b.
      Proof. Let r be the smallest non-negative number of the form a − b·q. If r ≥ b, then r − b would be a smaller one, a contradiction, so 0 ≤ r < b. If a = b·q + r = b·q′ + r′ with both remainders in [0, b), then b ∣ (r − r′) and |r − r′| < b, so r = r′ and q = q′. ∎
      Definition. gcd(a, b), the greatest common divisor, is the largest integer dividing both a and b. a and b are coprime if gcd(a, b) = 1.
      Theorem 3 (Euclid's algorithm). gcd(a, b) = gcd(b, a mod b).
      Proof. Write a = b·q + r. Any common divisor of a and b divides r = a − b·q, and any common divisor of b and r divides a = b·q + r. So the two pairs have exactly the same common divisors, and therefore the same greatest one. ∎

      Why it matters: repeating this step shrinks the numbers quickly until the remainder is 0, which computes gcd(e, φ(n)) efficiently. That's how we check the condition gcd(e, φ(n)) = 1.

      3Bézout's identity and the modular inverse

      Theorem 4 (Bézout's identity). For integers a and b, not both 0, there are integers x and y with a·x + b·y = gcd(a, b).
      Proof. Let g be the smallest positive number of the form a·x + b·y. Divide a by g: a = g·q + r with 0 ≤ r < g. Then r = a − (a·x + b·y)·q is also of the form a·x′ + b·y′, and it's smaller than g, so r = 0. Hence g ∣ a, and likewise g ∣ b, so g ≤ gcd(a, b). But gcd(a, b) divides a·x + b·y = g, so gcd(a, b) ≤ g. Therefore g = gcd(a, b). ∎
      Corollary 5 (modular inverse). If gcd(e, m) = 1, there is a unique d with 0 < d < m and e·d ≡ 1 (mod m). If gcd(e, m) > 1, no such d exists.
      Proof. By Bézout, e·x + m·y = 1 for some x and y, so e·x ≡ 1 (mod m); take d = x mod m. If two such numbers d and d′ existed, then d ≡ d·(e·d′) = (d·e)·d′ ≡ d′ (mod m), so they're equal. Conversely, if g = gcd(e, m) > 1 and e·d = 1 + m·k, then g divides e·d − m·k = 1, which is impossible. ∎

      Why it matters: this is exactly why RSA requires gcd(e, φ(n)) = 1: it guarantees that the private key d exists and is unique. The extended Euclidean algorithm, which keeps track of x and y while running Euclid's algorithm, computes d. For e = 3 and φ(n) = 40: 3 × 27 = 81 = 2 × 40 + 1, so d = 27.

      From now on, we use the defining property of d in this form: e·d = 1 + k·φ(n) for some integer k ≥ 0.

      4Euclid's lemma and two primes

      Theorem 6 (Euclid's lemma). If p is prime and p ∣ a·b, then p ∣ a or p ∣ b.
      Proof. Suppose p does not divide a. Since p is prime, its only divisors are 1 and p, so gcd(a, p) = 1. By Bézout, a·x + p·y = 1. Multiply by b: a·b·x + p·b·y = b. Both terms on the left are multiples of p (the first because p ∣ a·b), so p ∣ b. ∎
      Lemma 7 (two distinct primes). If p and q are distinct primes, p ∣ x and q ∣ x, then p·q ∣ x.
      Proof. Write x = p·t. Then q ∣ p·t. Since q is prime and q does not divide p (p is a different prime), Euclid's lemma gives q ∣ t, so t = q·u and x = p·q·u. ∎

      Why it matters: this is the final step of the main proof. We'll show that med − m is divisible by p and by q separately, and Lemma 7 combines them into divisibility by n = p·q. (This is the simplest case of the Chinese remainder theorem.) It's also the reason p and q must be distinct.

      5Fermat's little theorem

      Theorem 8 (Fermat's little theorem). If p is prime and p does not divide a, then
      ap−1 ≡ 1 (mod p)
      Proof. Consider the p − 1 numbers a·1, a·2, …, a·(p − 1), reduced mod p.
      • None is 0: if p ∣ a·i, Euclid's lemma gives p ∣ a or p ∣ i, but neither is true, since 1 ≤ i ≤ p − 1.
      • They're all different: if a·i ≡ a·j (mod p), then p ∣ a·(i − j), so p ∣ (i − j), which forces i = j because |i − j| < p.
      So these are the numbers 1, 2, …, p − 1 in some shuffled order. Multiplying them all together (Lemma 1):
      ap−1 · (p − 1)! ≡ (p − 1)! (mod p)
      Now (p − 1)! is not divisible by p (by Euclid's lemma, since none of its factors is), so gcd((p − 1)!, p) = 1, and by Corollary 5 it has an inverse mod p. Multiplying both sides by that inverse gives ap−1 ≡ 1 (mod p). ∎

      Why it matters: this is the engine of the proof. On a p-position clock, any power of the form a(p−1)·t collapses to 1. Example: 24 = 16 ≡ 1 (mod 5), and 710 ≡ 1 (mod 11). It fails for non-primes: 214 mod 15 = 4, not 1.

      6Euler's totient for n = pq

      Definition (Euler's totient). φ(n) is the number of integers from 1 to n that are coprime to n. In the session, this is the size of the "hidden clock."
      Theorem 9. If p and q are distinct primes, then φ(p·q) = (p − 1)(q − 1).
      Proof. Among 1, 2, …, pq, a number fails to be coprime to pq exactly when it's divisible by p or by q (any common factor greater than 1 has a prime factor r; since r divides p·q, Euclid's lemma says r divides p or q, so r is p or q). There are q multiples of p (p, 2p, …, qp) and p multiples of q. A number divisible by both is divisible by pq (Lemma 7), and only pq itself is in range, so it was counted twice. So the count of non-coprime numbers is p + q − 1, and
      φ(pq) = pq − p − q + 1 = (p − 1)(q − 1). ∎

      Why it matters: computing φ(n) this way requires knowing p and q. Anyone who could compute φ(n) from n alone could compute d, which is why RSA's security rests on factoring. For n = 55: φ(55) = 4 × 10 = 40.

      A note on Euler's theorem. Fermat's theorem generalizes to any modulus: if gcd(a, n) = 1, then aφ(n) ≡ 1 (mod n). Many textbooks prove RSA with it. But it only covers messages coprime to n. The proof below uses Fermat on each prime separately instead, which also handles the rare messages that share a factor with n, so it works for every m.

      7The main proof

      Theorem (RSA correctness). With p, q, n, e, d as in Section 0, for every integer m with 0 ≤ m < n: med ≡ m (mod n), and therefore (me mod n)d mod n = m.

      Step 1: rewrite the exponent. By the choice of d (Corollary 5) and Theorem 9:

      e·d = 1 + k·φ(n) = 1 + k·(p − 1)(q − 1) for some integer k ≥ 0

      Step 2: show med ≡ m (mod p). There are two cases.

      • Case A: p does not divide m. By Fermat's little theorem, mp−1 ≡ 1 (mod p). Then, using Lemma 1:
        med = m · mk(p−1)(q−1) = m · (mp−1)k(q−1) ≡ m · 1k(q−1) = m (mod p)
      • Case B: p divides m. Then p also divides med, so both sides are ≡ 0 (mod p), and med ≡ m (mod p) holds trivially.

      In both cases, p ∣ (med − m).

      Step 3: show med ≡ m (mod q). The same argument with p and q swapped (writing mk(p−1)(q−1) = (mq−1)k(p−1)) gives q ∣ (med − m).

      Step 4: combine. p and q are distinct primes that both divide med − m, so by Lemma 7, n = p·q divides it:

      med ≡ m (mod n)

      Step 5: conclude. Decryption computes cd mod n with c = me mod n. By Lemma 1, cd ≡ (me)d = med ≡ m (mod n). Since 0 ≤ m < n, m is the unique remainder in that range, so cd mod n = m exactly. ∎

      8Signatures: the same proof in reverse

      Corollary 10. For every 0 ≤ h < n: (hd mod n)e mod n = h.
      Proof. (hd)e = hde = hed, and the main proof never used which exponent came first. ∎

      Why it matters: this is what makes digital signatures work. A CA signs a fingerprint h with its private key, s = hd mod n, and anyone can check it with the public key: se mod n = h. It's the basis of certificates (Part 9.1) and server authentication (Part 9.2).
